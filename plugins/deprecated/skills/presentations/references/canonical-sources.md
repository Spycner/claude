# Canonical Sources

This is not a workflow step. It is a deeper-reading list for users who want to understand the research and craft tradition behind the defaults that the presentations skill enforces. The skill's host agent cites this file at the bottom of SKILL.md Key Principles so a reader can trace any default back to its source. Entries are attributed and priority-ordered. Specific data, quotations, and page numbers have been intentionally omitted; cite the work at the author, title, and year level only.

## Books, by priority

### Highest priority

### The Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto, _The Pyramid Principle_, 3rd ed (most recent). Top-down argument, answer-first communication, grouping, and MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) decomposition. Why it matters: foundational for executive decks where the audience expects the recommendation up front and the supporting argument structured as a pyramid of grouped, summarized ideas. Strengths: the canonical text on logical decomposition for business communication; the SCQA (situation, complication, question, answer) opening and the governing-thought-down structure underpin most modern consulting deck templates. Weaknesses: light on visual execution; says little about slide layout, typography, or chart design.

### Resonate

Nancy Duarte, _Resonate_, 2010. Audience-centered transformation, contrast between what-is and what-could-be, persuasion through narrative shape. Why it matters: brings story craft into business communication and gives the presenter a vocabulary for the emotional arc of a talk, not just its logical content. Strengths: the bridge between story design (acts, turns, calls to adventure) and the practical business presentation; the contrast pattern is one of the most-cited persuasion devices in modern presentation craft. Weaknesses: less formal than Minto on logical decomposition; a Duarte deck without a Minto skeleton can feel emotionally rich but argumentatively soft.

### Multimedia Learning

Richard Mayer, _Multimedia Learning_, 3rd ed. The cognitive-science foundation for restraint: the coherence principle (remove non-essential material), the redundancy principle (do not narrate the same words shown on screen), the signaling principle (cue attention to what matters), and the contiguity principle (place related text and graphics near each other in space and time). Why it matters: experimental backing for the design choices that other authors recommend on taste grounds. Strengths: every major principle is supported by controlled experiments; this is the empirical anchor for any argument about why fewer words on a slide actually help comprehension. Weaknesses: dense for a non-academic reader; the prose assumes familiarity with cognitive psychology terminology.

### Very high priority

### The Visual Display of Quantitative Information

Edward R. Tufte, _The Visual Display of Quantitative Information_, 2nd ed. The canonical text on statistical graphics, the data-ink ratio, small multiples, and graphical integrity (the lie factor). Why it matters: the standard reference for any slide that contains a chart, a table, or a quantitative comparison. Strengths: unmatched for analytical credibility; the chapters on graphical integrity remain the strongest defense against misleading visualization. Weaknesses: sparse on persuasion and delivery; Tufte's prescriptions are about truth-telling, not about moving an audience to a decision.

### Show Me the Numbers

Stephen Few, _Show Me the Numbers_, 2nd ed. A practical manual for designing tables and graphs that communicate quantitative information clearly to a business audience. Why it matters: where Tufte gives the theory, Few gives the recipe; chart-type-by-chart-type guidance with worked examples. Strengths: highly applied; the chart-selection framework (compare values, show distribution, show correlation, show part-to-whole, show change over time, show ranking) is the most useful single decision tool for data slides. Weaknesses: narrow scope outside data slides; says little about the rest of the deck.

### Beyond Bullet Points

Cliff Atkinson, _Beyond Bullet Points_, 4th ed. Storyboarding a presentation on paper before opening slide software, and aligning the spoken and visual channels so the words on the slide do not duplicate the words coming out of the speaker's mouth. Why it matters: an actionable workflow book that operationalizes the Mayer redundancy principle in a way a working presenter can adopt tomorrow. Strengths: the storyboard-first method is the strongest antidote to the build-as-you-go deck. Weaknesses: Microsoft-centric; examples and templates assume PowerPoint, though the method transfers to any tool.

### High priority

### Presentation Zen

Garr Reynolds, _Presentation Zen_, 3rd ed. Simplicity, restraint, presence, and the influence of Japanese aesthetic principles on slide design. Why it matters: a corrective against clutter; teaches the presenter to see white space as content. Strengths: visually persuasive in its own right; the before-and-after slide pairs are the most memorable demonstration of why restraint reads as authority. Weaknesses: less rigorous on executive-style argumentation; a Zen deck without a Minto skeleton can feel beautiful but unresolved.

### Storytelling with Data

Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, _Storytelling with Data_. Moving from raw analysis to audience-ready charts: choosing the right chart type, eliminating clutter, using pre-attentive attributes to direct attention, and telling a story with the data. Why it matters: applied and approachable; the most accessible entry point for a presenter who has analytical work to communicate but no formal training in visualization. Strengths: practical, exercise-driven, and grounded in real business examples. Weaknesses: more chart-centric than full-deck-centric; a Knaflic-trained presenter still needs Minto or Duarte for the surrounding argument.

### The Craft of Scientific Presentations

Michael Alley, _The Craft of Scientific Presentations_, 2nd ed. The strongest book on assertion-evidence structure for technical talks: every slide makes one assertion in a sentence-style headline at the top, supported by visual evidence (a chart, a diagram, a photograph) below, with bullet lists used sparingly. Why it matters: assertion-evidence is the most experimentally validated alternative to the topic-and-bullets default. Strengths: outstanding for evidence-led explanatory talks (research findings, engineering walkthroughs, scientific results). Weaknesses: science-talk slanted; the worked examples are drawn from physics, engineering, and the life sciences.

### Made to Stick

Chip Heath and Dan Heath, _Made to Stick_, 2007. The SUCCESs framework (simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, story) for making messages memorable. Why it matters: useful when survival outside the room is the concern: when the audience will repeat the message to others who did not see the deck. Strengths: vivid and quotable; the curse-of-knowledge chapter is the strongest single argument for designing decks around what the audience does not already know. Weaknesses: more communication theory than slide craft; gives the why and the what, but the how is left to the presenter.

## Primary papers and standards

**Garner and Alley, "How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension," 2013.** The empirical anchor for assertion-evidence: an experimental study finding that assertion-evidence slides improved comprehension and delayed recall, and reduced misconceptions and cognitive load, compared to PowerPoint-default topic-and-bullets slides. This is the paper to cite when defending the sentence-headline plus visual-evidence pattern against an audience that prefers the default.

**Mayer and Moreno, "Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in Multimedia Learning," 2003.** Lays out the dual-channel and limited-capacity assumptions of cognitive load theory and enumerates concrete design moves to reduce overload (off-loading, segmenting, pre-training, weeding, signaling, aligning, eliminating redundancy, synchronizing, individualizing). This is the theoretical basis for almost every later recommendation about reducing slide clutter.

**Mayer's chapter on coherence, signaling, redundancy, and contiguity.** Mayer's _Multimedia Learning_ contains chapters that summarize the major multimedia principles and the experimental tests that established each one. Cite this when a stakeholder asks where a specific design rule comes from; it is the bibliography behind the bibliography.

**Cleveland and McGill, "Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application to the Development of Graphical Methods," 1984.** Establishes that graph construction should use perceptual tasks high in an accuracy ordering: position along a common scale and length are decoded more accurately than angle and area, which are in turn decoded more accurately than color saturation and color hue. This is the empirical reason to prefer bar charts to pie charts when precise comparisons matter.

## Standards and official guidance

**WCAG 2.2 (W3C).** The authoritative accessibility baseline for digital content, applicable to presentation slides shared as files or projected with live captioning. The requirements most relevant to slides are a minimum 4.5:1 contrast ratio for normal text, a 3:1 ratio for large text and for meaningful non-text graphics, captions for any synchronized media embedded in the deck, and the rule that color must not be the only carrier of meaning (a chart that distinguishes series only by color fails; the same chart with distinct line styles or direct labels passes).

**TEDx Speaker Guide (public).** The publicly available speaker guidance from the TEDx organization, offering practical recommendations on idea development, slide simplicity, the one-point-per-graph rule, font-size minimums for talk-style slides shown to a live audience, and rehearsal discipline. The most useful single reference for a presenter preparing a keynote or conference-style talk who needs a credible external standard to point at.

## How to use this list

Read Minto for logic. Read Duarte for movement. Read Mayer for the cognitive evidence behind restraint. Read Tufte, Few, and Knaflic for data. Read Alley and Atkinson for slide craft. Read Reynolds for restraint as an aesthetic stance. The skill's defaults distill the consensus of these sources into a usable workflow, but they do not substitute for the books themselves; a presenter who reads even three of these (one from each cluster: argument, evidence, craft) will produce decks that no skill can produce on its own.
